# Why Are Negotiations Important

- They help you get paid for what you’re worth**
Negotiating helps you get the complete compensation package and get paid your market value.

- They help the employer understand the value you provide**
The employer needs to understand the value you will add to the company from the word go. Always communicate the project/task you are working on and your accomplishments.

- They ensure you are being treated equally as other employees in the company**
It is so easy to get exploited by both the employer and teammates if you do not negotiate. Make sure you are working on the right tasks and you get the rightful compensation package with all the perks/benefits.

- They save time and peace of mind**
As I always say, it’s better to wait for 2-3 months to get a better role than to close an offer you will regret.
# What Are Negotiations

They are the discussions you hold with the employer after passing the interview steps to settle on a compensation package and terms of operations.

In this module, we will get into details on what to expect during negotiations, what to do, what not to do, etc. I love this module because you will get to learn from my mistakes as well.

I know what it feels like to settle for a job you don’t like, being “used” by your teammates, and missing out on the fun part of working in a company. My goal is not only to help you land your next role but also to get paid your worth.

Let’s start with the general knowledge
# Things To Know About Negotiations

- Everything at once** - It’s better to negotiate all things at once so you can leverage other things that may be important to one party.
- Timing is everything **- It is important to know the big picture of the company
  - Did they just have a big loss? Employers are willing to give less if the company just took a huge hit
  - Did they lose a big contributor? They are more likely to save you while they are in scramble mode
  - Did they just get a big win or funding? People are more willing to spend when they just got a big payday
  - How long have they tried to solve a problem or have a position open?
  - When is there a fiscal year? Employers are willing to pay more at the beginning of the fiscal year than closer to the end since they could have under-budgeted
- Always take time **-  Don’t make any irrational decisions, take your time, and think it over with loved ones. Don’t feel pressured by their timeline
- No one leaves 100% satisfied** - The most important thing is that you both want to leave happy, it is a compromise
- Don’t mention value first** - The first person to give a **value **almost always loses
- Do frame first** - The first person to give the **frame **has an advantage
  - Be the first mover in establishing the right process: shape the terms of future engagement.
- Be firm on substance, flexible on structure** - “I am looking for something around $120k and I am flexible on how we get there”
- Address the logic of appropriateness** - Ask them what would someone do in this situation (in your shoes)
- Show Empathy** - Show empathy for their concerns allowing them to focus on finding solutions rather than continued advocacy
  - Create Slack for the other person
  - Resisting unfair demands on substance is easier if you push back early on unfairness in the process.
- Always Prepare** - Be the most prepared person in the room.
  - Know the facts, anticipate the arguments, and understand your weaknesses
  - Have a process strategy, don't just strategize the negotiation process; strategize the implementation process.
  - Leverage the social proof (data and statistics)
- Negotiate process before substance **- If all parties can’t agree on the process then it will be harder for the substance
  - Synchronize with the other side of the process.
  - Seek clarity and commitment to the process.
  - Normalize the process and encourage others to normalize it
  - A commitment to a very rigid process is not always possible
  - Keep a low priority for progress on individual issues, but a high priority for approving the final agreement.
- Reflect on progress** - Misalignment in the process can be a real deal-breaker. Ensure that there is an agreement about what has been accomplished and what the path ahead looks like as early as possible
- Give up control but clarify conditions - **In negotiations, you don’t need to control the process, the most important part is your conditions are known
  - This clarifies for the other side what is and isn’t important to you. Making their life easier keeps either side from “owning” and clinging to its preferred idea or approach
  - Help find a solution by encouraging multiple and often creative proposals
  - Process negotiations can become proxy wars for leverage and legitimacy.
  - Your willingness to incur costs in support of the process sends a credible signal of your commitment.
- Seek equality, not an advantage - **When standing firm on principle, seek equality, not advantage, and address any substantive concerns that are affected by your stance
- Don’t be Ambiguous** - Ambiguity contracts are not ideal but can be good if at a standstill
  - Commitments should be explicit, unambiguous, and informative
- Credibility matters** - Safeguard your credibility by following through on your commitments, even the small ones.
- Justify your offer but don’t **EVER **apologize
- Nothing is official until signed** - Do not make anything public, until signed, and give them time to talk to the higher up
  - Nothing is agreed until everything is agreed!
# (How)Preparing For The Ask

- Plan Agenda
- Think about how long negotiations will last.
- Who will be involved and in what capacity?
- What will be on the agenda, and in what order will the issues be discussed?
- Who will draft the initial offer?
- Will negotiations be public or private?
- When and how will progress be reported outside of negotiations?
- Given multiple parties or issues, will there be one negotiation track or many?
- Will all the parties be in the same room at the same time?
- Will negotiations take place face-to-face or via technology?
- How many meetings will be scheduled?
- How will major deadlocks or other problems be managed?
- Will there be outside observers or mediators?
- Will deadlines if any be binding or not
- What milestones might help build momentum and keep the process on track
- If the negotiations end with no deal when and how might parties reengage
- Who are the parties that need to ratify the deal and how much support is sufficient for the passage

# What To Do When There Is A Standstill:

So, you have negotiated and are almost coming to an agreement but you are both stuck on something you don’t want to lose. How do you go about that fairly?

The employer doesn’t understand why you don’t want to lose something, let’s say in my case I always wanted remote work with flexible work hours.

On the other hand, the employee doesn’t understand why the employer is holding dear to something. For example, coming to office (no remote/ hybrid positions)

Before you fix such a situation, it is important to know that no one leaves 100% satisfied, the aim is to leave happy.
So the idea is to frame the negotiations in such a way that both parties will not feel disadvantaged.

Be empathetic by putting yourself in the shoes of the employer. After you have looked at the situation from their perspective, think about how you can convince them in a way they won’t feel they are losing.

For example, if they insist that I have to go to the office, yet I want remote full-time, I can say something like, “I will be sending daily looms of my work progress and accomplishments.”

You see, it will give them surety that I will still get the work done.
Another example is if they insist they can’t afford your budget, you can propose a rate increase with time.

They might propose something like, “ We will pay you $75k this year and $110k next year.”

Or you can negotiate things like extra weeks of vacation or paid leave, etc.

Makes sense, right? Instead of being stuck, you can negotiate and compromise what you can accordingly.

To get unstuck, agree to a process that can be revised, or start negotiating substance in parallel with the process.

# Do Advantages Affect Future Engagement?

This is a critical lesson. If one side is stubborn or refuses to negotiate there will be resentment afterwards.

Example: Salary

Let’s say you overprice yourself and will only accept if that’s the offer. Then you will have a “target” on your head from day one, with really high expectations and everything you do will be put under a microscope. Thus, having resentment which won’t be a very enjoyable environment to work in. With that amount of stress of not allowing to fail, it will probably be worse for the employer in the long run too.

Now let’s say the employer is lowballing you and refuses to budge and you do accept the offer there will be resentment as you will always see yourself as undervalued, leading to, you not enjoying the work as much. Thus, this will lead to you not giving your best effort and again not being great for the employer in the long run.

You can see in these examples that both extremes are bad. Neither party should leave 100% satisfied.

# Common Mistakes

- Not negotiating
- Not preparing
- Not knowing your value
- Not expressing what you want or are looking for more
- Not finding out the budget
- Saying a value first
- Not knowing the logistics
  - E.g. If the salary is discussed in hours before you give an hourly rate, make sure you know how many hours you will be working per week
  - E.g. Taking lower pay for more stock but you can’t cash out the stocks until after 4 years
  - Not knowing what a definition is for the other parties

I made all of these (and more) mistakes during my job search and I have discussed them in the “Learn From Mistakes” lesson.

# What-If

Ideally, the goal is to always negotiate even when you are happy with the offer.
When you negotiate:
- You will get paid your worth
- The employer will clearly understand your value to the company
- You will be treated equally with other employees
- You will save time and get some peace of mind.
