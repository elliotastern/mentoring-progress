# Importance Of Designing Yourself As A Product

- Helps you to stay ahead of the competition**
Position yourself so that you can uniquely solve your employer's problems. You can do this by leveraging your strengths or taking advantage of opportunities to give yourself an advantage over your competitors.

- Helps employers to find you**
Design yourself well so job recruiters can’t stop knocking at your door. Be strategic and relevant, and you will effortlessly land top jobs.

- It puts out your personality**
Employers look for more than skills and experience; they also consider habits, behavior, talents, and a general work ethic. Your personality really counts towards that. Don’t be shocked if you have fewer skills than the rest but are still picked over by them.

- Employers can easily recommend you**
Know the value you provide to employers and what you want to get in turn. This will help you to always settle for the best and get the best from employers. Employers recommend you when they know you can get the job done and right.
# What Is Included In “Designing Yourself As A Product”

The product you’re selling is **yourself**! Your mindset, behavior, habits, communication, skills and talents, what it’s like to work with you, your work ethic, and much more.

When you hone the salesperson’s identity, you can acknowledge that there is always a buyer and a seller in every transaction, even in a job search. Remember you only need one yes to sell your product for ~ $100k/year.

When you sell a product, your goal is not the money but to make sure the buyer really needs and can fully utilize the product. Therefore, not every buyer will be the right buyer for your product, and it’s up to you to decipher the good from the bad before going through with a sale.

What I mean by this is, let’s say you recently started a business and you’re selling a product, your initial thought might be to try to sell to everyone and get as much money as you can, but what if you sell your product to the wrong buyer?

Maybe the buyer won’t be satisfied with the product and will give you a bad review. That bad review could hinder your chances of selling more products, right? (a.k.a you get fired, or your employer won’t give you a reference)

Now, let’s say the product has emotions and goals, and it wants to help the employer, but the employer never utilizes the product to its full potential. Or worse, the super unique product is undervalued and used as a coat rack or coffee table. The product is wasted for this buyer, and if it could, then it should change buyers, right?

This analogy makes you think that if you want “a job” or “the job,” do you want to sell yourself to any employer that doesn’t value you or utilizes you for your goals?

In the contract negotiation module, we will talk more about your value financially. For example, if you had 2 options
- You accept a “blah” job with low pay
Or
- Wait a month or two to get a great job where they value you

Which one is better for you long-term?

I don’t believe anyone should have regrets because there is always something to take away from every situation, but if I had a chance to redo my most recent employer situation, I would. That is because I decided to take a blah job where I was VERY undervalued in terms of money and task-wise instead of waiting a little longer.

I take full responsibility for my actions but I do wish I could go back and really look if an employer is the best match for me in terms of money, value, job task, team, mentors to learn from, values, goals, etc.

Waiting a little longer for a better-matched job may help your overall well-being in the long run. Just something to consider.

For this module and all future modules, I really want you to focus on selling yourself as a product and who the buyer is (the niche).

Often, we try to please everyone or employers, but that is not the ideal way to sell a product.

If you try to sell baby products to a bachelor, do you think they will buy them?

Most likely not, right?

Okay, so why would we sell your computer vision skills to someone who is looking for an NLP expert?

There is a famous saying, “Jack of all trades, master of none”. This saying referring to a generalist data scientist means are also claiming yourself as the master of none, which means you know a little about a lot and not a lot about a little. Since employers often have an exact goal of hiring you, such as helping out with computer vision, knowing a little about this goal by being a jack of all trades will not set you up as a great hire for the employer.

Going back to the baby products analogy, you may sell to a bachelor who’s an incoming parent, but that doesn’t mean it’s worth the salesperson’s effort and time.
This goes the same for what type of position you’re looking for or what best suits you.

If your skills and interests don’t match the job title you are interested in, that’s fine. Then, we will know what skills to work on.

Even though we usually don’t want to be generalist data scientists, there are some cases when being a data science general is fine.

# “When Should I Be A Data Scientist Generalist?”

You don’t need to know everything to get a data science job. All the listed requirements in the job description are just a wishlist. Again, if you know everything, you won’t be as excited as there will be no new challenges.

It is important to realize that even if you don’t have a job, picking a specialization and focusing on it might get you that job even faster.

Here is a quick table showing an example. Think of the employer's perspective who is trying to build a computer vision project. A specialist in computer vision would have more hours working in computer vision than a generalist with more years of experience and may, therefore, be a better candidate. This is crucial as standing out as a candidate coming into an industry can be a tough sell, as others have far more experience.

|  | Computer Vision (hours) | Regression (hours) | NLP (hours) | Classification (hours) | Regression (hours) | Reporting (hours) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| General Data Scientist with 5 years of experience | 500 |  |  |  |  |  |
| Data Scientist specializing in Computer Vision coming into the job market | 900 | 50 |  |  |  |  |

Search for a number of jobs and look for the skills listed in the job description. If you find that most of the skills you’re familiar with fall under a certain job title, that should be your niche. Sometimes, data science and analyst jobs are so close that if you find yourself in an undecided situation, lean towards data science.

Typically, if the position is for a data science consultant or a smaller company with several types of sub-field data science problems.

As a data science consultant, you will often work on short-term projects, so you need to know a little bit of everything.

Additionally, when you are in a small team/company you won’t necessarily…

…have a data engineer to do the data processing and querying

…have a data analyst to do the data analysis work needed

…have a database architect/admin to maintain the DB

…have a Machine learning engineer to do the in-depth model research

…have an MLOps Engineer to do the complete deployment and monitoring of the model

Thus, having a little bit of knowledge of each is great, but most of the time, it is not ideal.
# The Importance Of Having A Niche

- Fewer competitors**
Focusing on a small area makes it easier for you to stand out from the competition
- Greater expertise**
When you focus on a particular niche, you sharpen your skills in the area and become an expert. This in turn makes you attract potential clients
- Higher rates**
When you specialize in a particular niche, you may be able to charge higher rates for your services as you can offer a level of expertise or knowledge that is not readily available elsewhere
- Better marketing**
Marketing yourself to potential clients is easier when you have a niche.
- Greater job satisfaction**
When you focus on an area you are passionate about, you find work more fulfilling and enjoyable
Overall, focusing on a niche can help you to differentiate yourself in the market and offer unique value to your clients

# Product Market Fit

This is the extent to which a product (you) satisfies the needs and wants of a specific market.

We must find places where we can offer more value than alternative options. Unfortunately, we do not have a map showing where we can fit in. So, we must try to find these places by observing the market’s response.

A product with a strong product market fit addresses a clear need or problem in a way that is superior to other solutions currently available in the market. It also has a strong demand and a loyal customer base.

On the other hand, a product with a poor product-market fit may have features that are not relevant or useful to the target market, or it may not effectively solve the problems it was designed to address.

To achieve product-market fit, you should typically conduct market research, skill development, and testing to ensure that you meet the needs of the target market.

This may involve gathering feedback from customers/employers you have worked with before, making changes based on that feedback, and re-designing yourself until you can meet the market's needs.

Achieving product market fit is important for your success as it can help drive revenue, increase customer loyalty, and establish a strong brand reputation.

# Strengths

You need to know your strengths because we want to highlight them to the employers and ensure they know.

If you have done this before the interview when you are preparing for the interviews, you can use the job description as a cheat sheet to match what they are looking for to what you have.

You need to know how to utilize your strengths. Often, we underutilize our strengths because we are so fixated on what we don’t have, or even worse… we compare ourselves to others, which makes us feel even worse.
You most likely won’t find your strengths that impressive, but that doesn’t mean others won’t.
A very important quote to remember when you feel like you lack the skills is
“You can teach skills, you can’t teach behavior”
It will remind you that you can learn most of the skills needed in a job from the job, but they will rarely teach you about mindset, communication, and other internal behaviors.

# Weaknesses

# Weaknesses are Opportunities to grow

The great thing about weaknesses is that you have control over them. Imagine the advantage you have over other employees who shy away from weaknesses and never improve them versus you who find your weaknesses and improve on them. Weaknesses are also a great way to improve, as there is more room for growth in a weakness. Additionally, you need to know your weaknesses because without knowing them, you won’t know how to improve, learn, and grow in your career.
Remember that weaknesses slow down the process of reaching your goals. Instead of letting a weakness become a hindrance to achieving your goals, make improvements. It’s easier to continue the momentum of improvements than try to get out of a hole that weaknesses put you into.

How to address weaknesses when applying
Weaknesses are a great opportunity to show your ability to grow. Harvard Business Review states that the 3 main traits to hire are skill (for us, hard skills), interpersonal skills (ability to work with others), and the ability and willingness to improve. Our weaknesses are a great place to show our ability to improve and our self-awareness of our weaknesses. We can showcase the main component of a great employee with our weaknesses! Remember, every single person on earth has a weakness. Nobody is the best at everything.

Here is an example of a response we can give to show we can improve and have self-awareness with the age-old tough interview question:

Question: What is your greatest weakness?

Answer: I've recognized that public speaking isn't my strongest skill. To address this, I've been taking public speaking classes and seeking opportunities to present in front of groups. These efforts have already helped me feel more confident and articulate when addressing larger audiences.

As a hirer, this answer would be give a huge boost to my perception of the candidate.

In conversation, you must know how to flip your weaknesses to strengths as a mindset or perspective shift.
You don’t need to bring them up directly but when they do come up, you need to know how to handle them and not shy away. That way you will turn your weaknesses into positives and mention how you are working on them.
It’s all about improvements over perfection. If you think of your weaknesses, then most likely others will see or bring them up, and through this, you will prepare yourself to handle them.
The idea is to be optimistic and find the silver lining in every scenario.

Examples:

I lack a 4-year degree in data science => My 1-year data science Bootcamp has given me the same skills as someone with a 4-year degree. Great, I was efficient with my time and I saved 3 years!

My X working years are unrelated experience => My X years have taught me about the fast paced business, how to lead a team, and make critical decisions for my employer.

My X years of related experience, while I was in academia doesn’t count => Actually my time management was really good so that I could work a full-time job while doing full-time schooling.

You don’t have a formal education in coding => Ya exactly my coding skills are comparable to those who have formal education and I learned by myself, imagine what I can do on the job.

See, these are all objections employers could give you, but you turned them into positives and strengths to highlight why you are unique and a great candidate.

Being a salesperson is all about persuasion and shifting the perspective of the problem to get the buyer to see why the product is actually a great choice for them.

I had a mentor tell me once that “***There are no objections to selling, just concerns.”***

Think about it, they wouldn’t have interviewed you if you didn’t have the skills, that’s what the resume is for. The interview is to see if you are the right fit. So if they don’t actually have objections, they only have concerns (E.g. not enough degrees, experience, etc) and then disapprove of their concerns.

One of my favorite rebuttals for objections (I mean concerns 🙃) is

**Employer:** “You don’t have all the requirements for the job”

**Me:** “With all due respect, if I had all the requirements of the job then I wouldn’t have anything to work towards and I wouldn’t be the best candidate in the long-term as I could lose enjoyment with the lack of a challenge in my work. That’s why I was really excited for this role as I will get to learn and improve my skills.”

So think about the objections to your product and sell those silver linings!

# Values

Values are guidelines and standards of behavior.

When you sell yourself as a product, knowing your values and the employer’s core values is important so you can remind the buyer that their values align with yours.

What are your values?

It is great to reflect and compare if you have the same values as others/teams/employers. If you do, then great. Make sure you bring this up to the employer. If you don’t, maybe this isn’t the place for you (long-term).

Thus, this is one step closer to narrowing your niche to find the ideal buyer for your product!

# Goals

Goals are the results we want to achieve in life or the direction to always work towards.

To sell a product, you need to know your goal. What problem does the product solve, plus does it align with the buyer’s goal?

What are your goals? (Refer to module 1 exercise)

What do you want to learn, develop, and improve in your career?

What are your financial, emotional, physical, and mental goals?

If a company doesn’t train you, does that support your mental and career goals?

If a company says “it might be 80-hour weeks in the beginning because we are a start-up but it will be worth it in the long run” does that support your mental/emotional/physical goals?

If an employer doesn’t value work-life balance, does it align with your goals?

Does an employer support your career path/growth plan?

If the buyer’s goals don’t match the goals of the product would you still sell it or would you look for a better buyer?
