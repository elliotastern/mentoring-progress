# Why Are Technical Interview Rounds Important?

- It is an unbiased way to demonstrate your ability to perform the job**
Technical interviews improve that selection is based on facts and data.

- It helps to build your confidence**
There is no better solution to nail a technical interview than preparing. Preparing gives you some level of certainty that you are going to perform well.

- Technical interviews allow you to showcase any additional technical knowledge you have that might give you leverage**
Don’t forget the purpose of any interview is to sell yourself as a product. The more competitive advantages you have, the better the product

- Technical interviews give the employer the chance to evaluate the interviewee’s perspective on learning.**
Most employers appreciate employees who are willing to learn and overcome new challenges. If you pass the technical round, it avoids the subjective resume or lying of skills to determine if you are skilled enough for what they are looking for.

# What Are Technical Interviews?

Technical interviews are a set of questions given by experts to evaluate the candidate’s knowledge and skills in a particular field.

They are mostly used to check for:
- Technical skills and knowledge related to the job position**
- Problem-solving skills**
- Communication skills**
- Teamwork skills**
- Adaptability and flexibility**
- Learning and Growth Potential**

# Types Of Technical Rounds

There are two types of technical interviews
- Outside of interview technical rounds**
  - Assessments
  - Take-home project/assessments
- Live technical rounds **

## Assessments

### What are assessments?

They come in an exam format normally in a platform like HackerRank, and take 1 or 2 hours.
These are used mostly by large companies with 500+ employees.
They might not be necessarily fair tests for entry-level Data Scientists/Analysts, especially if you don’t come from a software engineering background but in my opinion, coding assessments are really important because they ensure selection is based on facts and data.
They often test on coding fundamentals.
For example for software-related roles (Not typically Data analysts)
- Data Structures & Algorithms (DSA)
- Immutable vs mutable
- Shallow vs Deep copy
- Time & space complexity
- etc

## Take-home assessments

## **The Employer gives you a dataset or a problem, then some questions or instructions**

- Typically you have somewhere between 1 to 7 days to return the assessment
- You should still understand or finish most of it but that is not as important as you think
- Structure:
  - Typically they give you a problem they are currently working on
- They are looking for:
  - Your communication
  - Your presentation skills
  - Your interpretation skills
  - Your technical skills
  - If you like the problem (aka if you like the work you would be doing)
  - Understanding the domain

### Each employer/team will have their Take-home assessments slightly differently.

They are relatively fun and I believe enjoyable.
If you don’t enjoy the take-home assignment, it normally breaks down to one of 4 things
- You don't have time and thus it makes it more stressful
- You don't feel confident enough thus making it more stressful. Although I would still try it, as it is a free chance to learn an industry-type problem
- You are worried about the assessment being **perfect **because you want a job now, thus making it more stressful
- The task is not **enjoyable. ** I recommend you still try it especially if you like the company and you have the time. Basically, it's an indicator of what the job would be like.
**NOTE: **Remember, the assessments represent the type of teammates you will have.
The harder the interview process, the more experienced and qualified your team will be, which means the more you can learn from others. This is something I learned after I got my last couple of jobs. If you don't have to pass a coding assessment, your team probably can’t code well either. If the data science assessment was more like an analyst position, it could mean the work or the team might be more like data analysts.

## Technical Interviews

**NOTE: **Most of these technical Interviews will be taught in video with examples in a worksheet [00 - Interview Prep Checklist - Worksheet](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1-3QlFR2AwNi0VhRfUkYwBErkT-iHGyTRvjC1RNeUUiI/edit?usp=sharing)

These types of interviews are very different for each company/team/employer.

Some types include
- Whiteboard coding (In-person or Virtually)
- Live coding (In-person or Virtually)
- Brain Teasers
- Case studies / Open-ended
- System Design (Mid-Senior positions)
- Basic Knowledge Tests (ie “What is overfitting?”)

For most of these, you will need to explain a problem or solution to the employer so that they understand your thinking and how you solve the problem.

Thinking aloud is an important skill to grasp here. Ideally, you should be able to explain your thinking and how you came to a conclusion.

### Whiteboard Coding (In-person or Virtually) - **Under Construction**

This involves writing a sudo code for a solution. These interviews are common for companies/teams that have a more mature data science team and in more software-related roles (DE, MLE, MLOps, SWE).

If you have a software engineering background you have probably heard of the famous book [Cracking the coding interview](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/54845619-cracking-the-coding-interview), which basically teaches how to go about live coding/problems in interviews.

**NOTE:** Whatever you learn from the book will be very helpful (there are lots of free resources to use if you don’t want to buy the book)

- Don’t Repeat Yourself (DRY) approach for coding
- Keep It Stupid Simple (KISS) approach for any planning
- Edge cases for coding and tech interviews
- Time complexity
- Memory complexity
- Brute force solution
- Talk out a solution/talk about what you are thinking
- [UMPIRE approach](https://guides.codepath.com/compsci/UMPIRE-Interview-Strategy) for coding assessments, coding or tech interviews
- Understand** what the interviewer is asking for by using test cases and questions about the problem.
- Match** what this problem looks like to known categories of problems, e.g. Linked List or Dynamic Programming and strategies or patterns in those categories.
- Plan** the solution with appropriate visualizations and pseudocode.
- Implement** the code to solve the algorithm.
- Review** the code by running specific example(s) and recording values (watchlist) of your code's variables along the way.
- Evaluate** the performance of your algorithm and state any strong/weak or future potential work.

### This method aims at evaluating your:

- Coding Knowledge
- Communication
- Data Structures and Algorithms (DSA)
- Design
- Efficiency

### Live coding (In-person or Virtually)

**NOTE: **Some interviews will say living coding and they will refer to the previous section of Whiteboarding coding problems or explained below, prepare for both

This type will actually assess your coding skills live and will expect good practice, syntax, and an efficient working solution.

It's not common for data analysts or data scientists (Big tech companies will still expect Data Analyst and Data Scientists to do this)

Compared to the whiteboard coding, many of the practices are the same.

### **Evaluation: **

- Coding Knowledge
- Communication
- Data Structures & Algorithms (DSA)
- Code/Problem Design
- Efficiency
